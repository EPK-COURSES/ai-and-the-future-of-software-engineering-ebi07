AI Statistics and Industry Reports

1. Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become one of the most influential technologies in software engineering. Tools such as GitHub Copilot, ChatGPT, and AI-powered development assistants are changing how developers write code, debug applications, create documentation, and learn new technologies.

The purpose of this research is to examine statistics, surveys, and industry reports from organizations such as Stack Overflow, GitHub, McKinsey, and Deloitte in order to understand how AI is being adopted in software engineering and what impact it is having on developers and businesses.

2. Background Information

Artificial intelligence (AI) is technology that enables computers and machines to simulate human learning, comprehension, problem solving, decision making, creativity and autonomy.

Applications and devices equipped with AI can see and identify objects. They can understand and respond to human language. They can learn from new information and experience. They can make detailed recommendations to users and experts. They can act independently, replacing the need for human intelligence or intervention (a classic example being a self-driving car).

But in 2024, most AI researchers, practitioners and most AI-related headlines are focused on breakthroughs in generative AI (gen AI), a technology that can create original text, images, video and other content. To fully understand generative AI, it’s important to first understand the technologies on which generative AI tools are built: machine learning (ML) and deep learning.

- How AI assists developers

Artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing the software development process by introducing tools and techniques that enhance productivity, accuracy and innovation. From automating code generation to optimizing testing and deployment, AI is reshaping how software is designed, built and maintained.

AI, particularly generative AI (gen AI) and large language models (LLMs), streamline the development cycle by automating key steps, from idea generation and requirement gathering to coding and testing.

Operating in collaboration with human developers, gen AI transforms ideas into requirements. It then converts those requirements into user stories, basic explanations of software features written from the perspective of the end user and generates test cases, code and documentation. This collaboration speeds up the development process and improves the quality of the final product.

AI has a significant impact on code generation. Machine learning-enabled tools use natural language processing (NLP) to interpret natural language descriptions and produce code suggestions or complete code. This capability accelerates coding, reduces human error and allows developers to focus on more complex and creative tasks rather than boilerplate code.

Beyond coding, AI technologies enhance debugging and testing. Advanced AI tools can automatically detect bugs, vulnerabilities and inefficiencies and suggest fixes or optimizations. AI-driven testing systems generate adaptive test cases and prioritize the most critical tests, improving software quality and security.

AI assists in project management and DevOps by automating routine tasks, improving time estimates and optimizing continuous integration/continuous deployment (CI/CD) pipelines. AI-driven tools help allocate resources, schedule tasks more efficiently and monitor system performance in real time, optimizing deployment and preventing potential failures.

Overall, AI is increasing development speed and accuracy and fostering a more reliable and secure software environment. The future will bring even more advancements. As gen AI evolves, it might fundamentally reshape every stage of development and might even render agile methodologies, as we know them today, obsolete.

- Examples of AI use in coding

AI-powered automation transforms twelve critical development workflows. GitHub Copilot users show 55% faster task completion rates, AI-assisted code review improves efficiency by 10-30%, and Meta's Sapienz generates bug reports where 75% result in fixes. The critical success factor isn't adopting all twelve use cases. It's selecting the 2-3 that address your team's specific bottlenecks and measuring their impact on concrete metrics.

Senior engineers face recurring bottlenecks: code reviews consuming 15-20 hours per week, new developer onboarding taking 3-6 months for productivity, and technical debt accumulating faster than teams can address it. The problem isn't lack of tools. It's the absence of intelligent automation that understands code context well enough to deliver productivity gains without creating workflow friction.

3. AI Adoption Rates

AI adoption among software developers has grown at a remarkable pace between 2023 and 2025. Data from multiple major industry surveys tell a consistent story: the majority of developers are now using or actively planning to use AI tools, and adoption is still accelerating.

- Stack Overflow Developer Survey Findings

The Stack Overflow Developer Survey is one of the most widely cited annual surveys in the software industry, drawing responses from tens of thousands of developers worldwide. Its data on AI adoption provides a reliable, year-over-year benchmark.
    -	2023: 70% of developers reported using or planning to use AI tools in their development work.
    -   2024: 76% were using or planning to use AI tools — an increase from 70% the previous year. More notably, the share actively using AI tools (rather than just planning to) jumped from 44% to 62% in a single year. Among those using AI tools, 82% were using them specifically for writing code, making this the single most popular application (Stack Overflow, 2024).
    -	2025: 80% of developers now use AI tools in their workflows — an all-time high. However, this increased usage has not translated into increased confidence. Trust in AI accuracy actually fell from 40% in 2024 to just 29% in 2025, while the share of developers who actively distrust AI outputs rose to 46% (Stack Overflow, 2025).

These trends reveal an important pattern: adoption is driven by utility and curiosity, not necessarily confidence. Developers are integrating AI tools into their work at increasing rates, but they are simultaneously becoming more critical and cautious about the quality of AI-generated outputs. This is a healthy and expected maturation of any new technology category. It is also worth noting that the 2025 survey found 52% of developers either do not use AI agents or stick to simpler AI tools, and 38% have no plans to adopt agents. The more autonomous, “agentic” tier of AI tools has not yet achieved mass adoption, even as basic AI assistants have become mainstream.

- GitHub Octoverse and Copilot Data

GitHub’s annual Octoverse report and its published Copilot usage data offer another lens on AI adoption in software development.
    -	GitHub Copilot reached 20 million cumulative users by July 2025, up from roughly 5 million in early 2024 — a 400% year-over-year growth rate.
    -	More than 50,000 organisations actively use Copilot, and the tool is deployed at approximately 90% of Fortune 100 companies.
    -	The Octoverse 2025 report found that 50% of open source projects now have at least one maintainer using GitHub Copilot.
    -	On average, GitHub Copilot generates 46% of the code written by its users, with Java developers seeing this figure rise to 61%.

These figures demonstrate that AI coding assistance is not a niche or experimental activity: it is now a core part of mainstream software development at both individual and enterprise scale.

- Enterprise-Level Adoption: McKinsey Data

McKinsey’s annual State of AI surveys track adoption across entire organisations, providing a broader business perspective that complements the developer-focused surveys above.
    -   In early 2024, 65% of organisations reported regularly using generative AI — nearly double the figure from ten months prior (McKinsey, 2024).
    -	By 2025, 78% of organisations used AI in at least one business function, up from 55% in 2023 (McKinsey, 2025). 
    -	The average organisation using gen AI is deploying it across two business functions, most commonly in marketing/sales and product development.
    -	Despite near-universal awareness (99% of executives know about generative AI), only 1% of organisations report having reached a “mature” level of AI deployment.The gap between awareness and execution remains wide.

4. Productivity Improvements

One of the most important questions about AI tools in software engineering is whether they actually make developers more productive. The data here is broadly positive, though it comes with important nuances.

- Developer-Level Productivity Gains

The most widely cited productivity statistic comes from a GitHub-sponsored controlled experiment involving 4,800 developers. In the study, developers using GitHub Copilot completed a representative coding task 55% faster than those working without AI assistance (completing the task in an average of 1 hour 11 minutes, compared to 2 hours 41 minutes for the control group). In addition, 78% of Copilot users successfully completed the task, versus 70% in the control group.

Survey-based evidence reinforces this picture. According to GitHub data, 88% of developers using Copilot say they complete their tasks faster, and 96% report that repetitive coding tasks specifically are completed more quickly. A further 73% say they are better able to maintain a productive “flow state” when using Copilot. The 2025 Stack Overflow survey found that 52% of developers who use AI agents agree those tools have had a positive effect on their productivity.

It is worth separating objective and subjective productivity. Controlled studies measure objective output (tasks completed, time taken). Surveys capture how developers feel about their own productivity. Both types of evidence point in the same direction, but self-reported gains can overstate reality if developers are simply more satisfied with AI tools without producing objectively more or better work.

- Code Quality Improvements

A November 2024 GitHub research study specifically measured the quality of AI-assisted code across multiple dimensions. The findings showed that code written with Copilot assistance was measurably better across several dimensions. 
    -   Readability improved by 3.62%, with 13.6% more lines of code written without readability errors.
    - 	Reliability improved by 2.94% and maintainability improved by 2.47%.
    -	Conciseness increased by 4.16%.
    -	Code approval rates in peer review rose by 5%, meaning AI-assisted code is ready to merge more often on first submission.

However, the same research noted a significant concern: 29.1% of Python code generated by Copilot contains potential security weaknesses. This finding has led many organisations to implement mandatory human review processes for all AI-generated code, which partially offsets the raw speed gains but is considered essential for maintaining software security standards.

- Organisational-Level Productivity: The Scaling Problem

At the organisational level, the productivity story is more complicated. McKinsey’s 2025 State of AI research found that despite near-universal deployment, 94% of companies report not yet seeing “significant” value from their AI investments. This creates what McKinsey analysts describe as an echo of the historical “Solow Paradox” — the observation that transformative technologies often appear everywhere except in the productivity statistics for years after widespread adoption.

McKinsey identifies the root cause as a “scaling gap.” Two-thirds of companies remain stuck in pilot mode, running small experiments without achieving enterprise-wide transformation. Only around 6% of organisations are classified as AI high performers — those that attribute more than 5% of their EBIT to AI and report significant measurable value. The key differentiator for these high performers is not spending more money, but redesigning workflows rather than simply bolting AI models onto existing processes.

Deloitte’s 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise report (surveying leaders in August–September 2025) found that worker access to AI rose by 50% in 2025, and that twice as many leaders are now reporting that AI is delivering on efficiency and productivity promises compared to the prior year. The number of companies with 40% or more projects in production is expected to double within six months of the survey.

5. Developer Opinions

Beyond adoption rates and productivity metrics, understanding how developers feel about AI tools provides crucial context for predicting how the technology will evolve and where resistance may emerge.

- Enthusiasm and Confidence Over Time

Developer sentiment toward AI tools has followed a recognisable arc: early enthusiasm, followed by a more critical assessment as real-world experience accumulates. Stack Overflow’s surveys track this precisely:
    -	In 2023, more than 70% of developers expressed favourable views toward AI tools.
    -	In 2024, 72% remained favourable — a slight dip, which Stack Overflow attributed to developers encountering disappointing results in actual use.
    -	By 2025, positive sentiment had fallen further, to just 60%. Notably, professional developers (61% favourable) maintained slightly higher regard than those still learning to code (53%).

Despite falling enthusiasm, developers continue adopting AI tools at increasing rates. This tells us that practical utility trumps sentiment: even developers who are sceptical of AI output accuracy still find the tools useful enough to keep using. The analogy might be a developer who dislikes autocorrect but still finds it faster to leave it on.

- Trust in AI Accuracy

Trust in AI output accuracy is declining sharply, even as usage rises. In 2025, Stack Overflow found that 46% of developers actively distrust the accuracy of AI tools, while only 33% trust it — and a mere 3% report “highly trusting” AI outputs. This is a reversal from previous years when trust and distrust were roughly balanced.

This growing scepticism is not irrational. As developers use AI tools for more varied and complex tasks, they encounter more edge cases where AI generates plausible-sounding but incorrect or insecure code. The 29.1% potential security weakness rate in Copilot-generated Python code (mentioned in Section 4) is a concrete example of why experienced developers are right to maintain critical oversight.

Developers show particularly strong resistance to using AI for high-responsibility tasks. According to the 2025 Stack Overflow survey, 76% of developers do not plan to use AI for deployment and monitoring, and 69% do not plan to use it for project planning. The areas where AI is most accepted — writing code, searching for answers, writing documentation — are those where errors are more easily caught and corrected by human reviewers.

- Concerns About Job Security

A persistent question around AI in software engineering is whether it will displace developers. As of 2025, the majority of developers remain unworried. The 2025 Stack Overflow survey found that 64% of developers do not see AI as a threat to their jobs, though this figure has fallen from previous years, indicating a gradual increase in concern. The emerging consensus seems to be that AI handles routine and repetitive coding tasks, freeing developers to focus on architecture, problem definition, and complex reasoning — the work that is hardest to automate.

The concept of “vibe coding” — generating entire applications from natural language prompts with minimal manual coding — has attracted attention but remains marginal in professional settings. Nearly 72% of professional developers say it is not part of their professional work, and an additional 5% emphatically reject it.

6. Company Investments in AI

The scale of corporate investment in AI is enormous and growing rapidly, reflecting both genuine belief in AI’s transformative potential and competitive pressure not to fall behind.

- Market Size and Growth

    -	The global AI market reached approximately $391 billion in 2024 and is projected to exceed $1.8 trillion by 2030, representing a compound annual growth rate of approximately 37%.
    - 	The AI coding tools market specifically reached $7.37 billion in 2025 (up from $4.91 billion in 2024), with GitHub Copilot holding approximately 42% market share. This sub-market is projected to reach $30.1 billion by 2032.
    -	Over 90% of executives report positive ROI from their AI investments, and 92% plan to increase AI spending. However, McKinsey cautions that fewer than one-third of firms follow best practices such as tracking KPIs or establishing clear roadmaps for AI initiatives.

- Enterprise Deployment Patterns

McKinsey’s 2024 analysis found that gen AI high-performing organisations are deploying the technology across an average of three business functions, compared to two for typical firms. High performers are also more likely to invest in custom model development rather than relying solely on off-the-shelf tools, and are far more likely to use AI across risk, legal, and compliance functions — areas where most companies remain cautious.

Deloitte’s research on tech companies at the forefront of AI adoption found they are twice as likely as their conservative peers to say generative AI is transforming their organisation now or will do so within the next year. These early movers are investing heavily in reorganising developer experiences and IT workflows to get the best results from AI tools, rather than simply purchasing licences and hoping for the best.

According to Deloitte’s Q3 2024 State of Generative AI in the Enterprise report, 56% of organisations identify establishing a governance framework for gen AI as their top priority for mitigating risk. This reflects a maturing approach to AI adoption: investment is no longer just about buying tools, but about building the structures and safeguards to use them responsibly at scale.

7. Future Predictions

Industry analysts and major research organisations have published numerous predictions about where AI in software engineering is headed. The near-term picture is one of deepening integration, with longer-term predictions pointing to fundamental changes in how software is built.

- Economic Potential

McKinsey’s modelling estimates that generative AI could contribute between $2.6 trillion and $4.4 trillion annually to the global economy across 63 identified use cases, with the highest concentrations of value in customer operations, marketing and sales, software engineering, and R&D. To put this in perspective, $4.4 trillion exceeds the entire GDP of the United Kingdom in 2021. Software engineering is consistently identified as one of the top four value-creation domains.

However, McKinsey cautions that capturing this value requires companies to move from running pilots to actually scaling AI across their organisations — a transition that the majority of firms have not yet completed.

- AI Agents: The Next Frontier

AI agents — autonomous software entities that can plan, reason, and take sequences of actions with minimal human input — are widely seen as the next major step beyond simple AI coding assistants. The transition from chat-based AI to agentic AI is already beginning but has not yet reached mainstream status.
    -	Gartner predicts that 40% of enterprise applications will feature task-specific AI agents by 2026, up from less than 5% in 2025.
    -	However, Gartner also predicts that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end of 2027, suggesting significant experimentation and failure ahead before successful patterns emerge.
    -	Deloitte predicts 2026 will see the gap between AI’s promise and reality begin to narrow, as organisations make further progress towards scaling AI from pilots to full production deployment.
    -	Deloitte’s enterprise AI infrastructure survey projects that the share of organisations with 31 or more AI production-ready use cases will grow from 44% in 2025 to 67% by 2028.

- Risks and Considerations

The future of AI in software engineering is not without significant risk. Several concerns stand out from the research literature:
    -	Security vulnerabilities: AI-generated code is increasingly implicated in security incidents. Deloitte’s 2026 Software Industry Outlook estimated that between March 2024 and February 2025, 16% of data breaches involved attackers using AI — primarily through AI-generated phishing and deepfakes. This share is expected to increase as AI attack tools proliferate.
    -	Over-reliance: As developer trust in AI accuracy declines (from 40% in prior years to 29% in 2025), the risk is not just that developers trust AI too much, but that inconsistent oversight — sometimes reviewing carefully, sometimes not — creates unpredictable quality levels.
    -	Energy consumption: Deloitte’s 2025 TMT Predictions forecast that global data centre electricity consumption will double to 4% of total global electricity by 2030, driven substantially by AI workloads. The environmental cost of AI at scale is a growing concern.
	-   Governance gaps: The top three reasons developers and organisations reject AI tools are security or privacy concerns, prohibitive pricing, and the availability of better alternatives. The absence of AI is ranked last — meaning the tools must earn their place by addressing real concerns, not simply by existing.

8. Interpretation and Discussion

The statistics presented in this report are not just numbers, they tell a story about how a powerful new category of technology is being integrated into one of the world’s most important professions. Several themes emerge from interpreting the data as a whole.

Adoption is driven by utility, not enthusiasm. The fact that AI tool adoption continues to rise (from 70% in 2023 to 80% in 2025) even as developer trust and positive sentiment both decline is a significant finding. It suggests that developers are using these tools not because they are impressed by them, but because they provide genuine practical value even when imperfect. This is actually a sign of maturity: rational adoption based on utility rather than hype.

The scaling gap is the real challenge for organisations. Individual developers can adopt AI tools incrementally and benefit immediately. Organisations face a harder challenge: integrating AI into complex workflows, managing governance and security, retraining staff, and measuring results rigorously. The fact that only 6% of organisations qualify as AI high performers despite near-universal awareness suggests that technology alone is insufficient, leadership, process redesign, and measurement are equally important.

Security is an underappreciated risk. The finding that nearly 30% of AI-generated Python code contains potential security weaknesses is alarming. In a world where AI is generating an increasingly large share of all production code, this implies that security review processes must scale proportionally. Organisations that adopt AI for speed without investing in security review infrastructure may be creating significant technical debt and vulnerability exposure.

The future belongs to teams that redesign workflows. McKinsey’s research is consistent in finding that the highest-performing organisations are not those that spend the most on AI, but those that fundamentally redesign their processes to leverage AI’s strengths. Bolting an AI tool onto an unchanged workflow captures a fraction of the potential value. Rebuilding the workflow around AI capabilities, as the leading companies are doing, is where the large productivity gains are found.

9. Conclusion

AI has moved from the periphery to the centre of software engineering in the space of just two to three years. The statistics from Stack Overflow, GitHub, McKinsey, Deloitte, and Gartner converge on a clear picture: AI tools are now used by the majority of developers globally; they provide measurable speed and quality improvements at the individual level; and they have the potential to generate trillions of dollars in economic value at scale.

At the same time, the data reveals important cautions. Developer trust in AI accuracy is declining even as usage rises. Most organisations have not yet figured out how to capture value from AI at enterprise scale. Security risks in AI-generated code are real and growing. And the most autonomous tier of AI tools, agents, remains far from mainstream adoption.

The overall trajectory, however, is clearly toward deeper and more capable AI integration in software development. The developers and organisations who will benefit most are those who approach AI with critical realism — using tools for what they do well, maintaining rigorous human oversight for high-stakes decisions, investing in governance and security infrastructure, and continuously measuring outcomes rather than simply assuming that adoption leads to value.
